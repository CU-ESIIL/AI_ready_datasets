Scientific datasets are increasingly being transformed into numerical embeddings on the assumption that learned representations make information more accessible and useful to artificial intelligence. Yet this assumption deserves renewed scrutiny as AI agents become capable of interpreting metadata, executing scientific code, and working directly with complex observations. Recent evaluations demonstrate that pretrained Earth embeddings do not consistently outperform original imagery or conventional scientific features (Ma et al., 2026), while the PANGAEA benchmark similarly finds that geospatial foundation models do not consistently outperform supervised alternatives (Marsocci et al., 2025). Meanwhile, research on agentic data retrieval demonstrates that machine-actionable semantic metadata substantially improves access to usable scientific data (Chen et al., 2026), and evaluations of scientific literature retrieval show that conventional keyword-based methods can outperform learned retrievers within agent workflows (Hu et al., 2026). Together, these findings challenge the assumption that increasingly sophisticated representations necessarily produce more capable scientific systems. They raise a fundamental question for the design of AI-ready datasets: What additional scientific value does an embedding provide when an intelligent agent already has access to the original observations, their scientific meaning, and the computational tools needed to analyze them? We propose to answer this question by evaluating representations within complete scientific workflows rather than measuring embedding quality solely through downstream prediction accuracy. Our central hypothesis is that rich, machine-actionable metadata and capable agents reduce the need for general-purpose embeddings, while specialized learned representations remain valuable when they capture transferable relationships or substantially reduce repeated computation.

We will use Fire VASE, an existing framework for representing the developmental histories of wildfires, as our primary experimental system (Tuff et al., manuscript in preparation). Fire VASE transforms sequences of observed daily burned-area growth into standardized developmental profiles, allowing fires to be compared by when their growth occurred rather than simply by final area, duration, or maximum growth rate. Its validated analysis includes 10,246 fires with at least three consecutive daily observations, represented through 20 normalized growth-allocation bins. The first five principal components explain approximately 89% of the standardized variation, demonstrating that much of the observed developmental structure can be summarized compactly. However, extensive validation also shows that low-dimensional compression occurs in constrained null histories, that local neighborhoods are less stable than broad developmental gradients, and that weather explains relatively little of the variation in developmental shape. These findings connect to broader concerns about dimensionality reduction: Liu et al. (2025) demonstrate that widely used neighbor-embedding methods can introduce artificial clusters and misleading geometric relationships, emphasizing the need to validate latent-space structure rather than interpret it at face value. Fire VASE is therefore particularly valuable because we already understand what its scientific representation preserves, what it excludes, and which apparent patterns require skepticism. We will compare four approaches using identical underlying observations: agents operating directly on dated fire histories, agents using scientifically interpretable VASE features, systems using statistical or neural embeddings, and adaptive agents that select among these approaches. This comparison will distinguish the value of representing fire development from the additional value of learning and storing an embedding.

Our first experiment will determine whether improved scientific metadata reduces the advantage of embeddings. We will construct progressively richer descriptions of the same fire observations, ranging from basic file and variable definitions to complete information about measurement uncertainty, temporal support, missing observations, provenance, and validated analytical operations. This experiment builds directly on Chen et al. (2026), who found that semantic metadata improves the precision of retrieving actionable datasets, but extends that work from dataset discovery to scientific computation and inference. Using the same agent models and controlled computational budgets, we will evaluate each representation strategy on independently verified tasks involving exact growth calculations, retrieval of developmentally similar fires, cross-event comparisons, and scientific prediction. A second experiment will investigate whether agent capability changes these relationships by comparing fixed analytical workflows, agents that select existing operations, and agents that construct and validate new computations. The central measurement will be the interaction between metadata quality, agent capability, and the incremental value of embeddings. If agents equipped with complete scientific semantics can reproduce the benefits of embeddings through direct computation, we will have identified conditions under which precomputed representations are unnecessary. Conversely, persistent embedding advantages would indicate that learned representations contribute useful statistical structure or computational efficiency beyond data accessibility. We will separately account for the costs of producing metadata, training models, constructing embeddings, and repeatedly querying the archive, including comparisons against conventional indexes and cached scientific computations.

Our second set of experiments will investigate where specialized embeddings improve scientific inference and where they introduce misleading structure. We will train temporal representations of fire development and progressively extend them to include spatial growth geometry, fragmentation, perimeter complexity, and environmental conditions. Their performance will be evaluated against raw observations, conventional scientific features, and the existing VASE morphospace on developmental similarity, independently observed outcomes, and prediction of subsequent growth. Forecasting experiments will use only information available through the prediction date, preventing completed fire histories from leaking future information into the representation. Geographic, temporal, and observation-depth holdouts will test whether learned relationships transfer beyond the fires used for training, addressing the generalization limitations documented in geospatial foundation-model evaluations (Marsocci et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026). We will also extend Fire VASE’s existing null-model framework to test scientific validity directly: preserving daily growth magnitudes while disrupting their temporal ordering, systematically removing observations from well-sampled fires, and comparing fires with similar developmental timing but different spatial organization. These tests will evaluate whether embeddings respond to meaningful changes in fire behavior, remain appropriately stable under scientifically irrelevant transformations, or instead organize fires according to sampling artifacts and mathematical constraints. Building on the reliability concerns identified by Liu et al. (2025), we will quantify the stability and scientific fidelity of learned neighborhoods rather than interpreting visually compelling clusters as evidence of distinct fire types or underlying mechanisms. Any apparent improvement in latent-space structure will require validation against independent scientific properties.

The final experiment will turn these findings into a generalizable method for deciding when embeddings are worth using. We will develop an adaptive representation-selection system that evaluates the scientific question, available observations, metadata, computational budget, and expected reuse before choosing among direct analysis, interpretable features, existing embeddings, or newly constructed representations. Its performance will be compared with systems committed to a single representation, measuring scientific correctness, predictive skill, uncertainty calibration, computational cost, reproducibility, and performance on questions outside the representation’s original training objectives. We will use these results to construct a representation-utility phase diagram identifying the conditions under which direct access, specialized embeddings, or adaptive combinations provide the greatest benefit, and then test whether those boundaries generalize to another environmental dataset. This extends existing work on representation benchmarking (Marsocci et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026), semantic data access (Chen et al., 2026), agent-mediated retrieval (Hu et al., 2026), and embedding reliability (Liu et al., 2025) into a unified framework for evaluating representation choice within scientific workflows. The intended contribution is both theoretical and practical: a falsifiable account of how representation value depends on scientific questions, information preservation, learned knowledge, and computational constraints, accompanied by a reproducible benchmark for evaluating those relationships. The broader implication is a different architecture for AI-ready science: datasets should preserve trustworthy observations and executable scientific meaning as their foundation, while embeddings become optional, testable computational instruments whose value must be demonstrated for the questions they are intended to answer.
